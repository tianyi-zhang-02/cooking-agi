# Tech interviews: how I practise LeetCode

[中文](leetcode.md) · **English**

> Reading time: ~2 min · Last reviewed: 2026-09

> **Read this first**: interview formats and emphases change fast, so this is time-sensitive; it describes only my own practice routine, with no company's questions or process.

The short version: **there is no shortcut with algorithm questions; you have to practise.** I also have no secret method to share — there are plenty of guides and tutorials online that do this better. This is simply what I do.

## What to practise

- **Hot 100 / 150**: the easiest place to start, and it covers most of the common question types.
- **Company-tagged questions**: close to an interview, working through a company's tag is good value.

Whether to pay for premium, and how much to prepare, depends on you. My own attitude is that more is better; if there is time, do some.

## How far to take it

LeetCode is more of a **hard gate**: clear the bar and that is enough :)

Unless you come from competitive programming, there is no need to chase perfection here — it has little to do with the actual job. Save some of that time for projects, system design, and fundamentals.

My own difficulty mix looks like a bell shape:

- **mediums are the bulk of it**, that is the main battleground;
- **easies are mostly for learning a pattern**; once mediums click, easies go quickly;
- **hards I do far fewer of** — they take a long time and a lot of mental energy.

## My routine

Simple, one question type at a time:

1. pick a type and do a run of questions in it;
2. when stuck, **read someone else's implementation first**, copy it out once, and make sure you understand it;
3. **come back a few days later and do it again**, writing it yourself this time;
4. when something will not click, have ChatGPT or Claude walk you through it; it is much faster than grinding alone.

Step 3 is the one that matters. Following a solution is not the same as knowing it; writing it yourself days later is.

If you practise in Python, go through [the Python you actually use in coding problems](python.en.md) first; it saves a lot of time.

## The list of types

Go through these one at a time and you have covered most of it:

- arrays, two pointers, sliding window
- hash maps, prefix sums
- binary search
- sorting and interval problems
- linked lists
- stacks and queues (including monotonic stacks)
- binary trees and recursion
- BFS / DFS and graphs
- backtracking
- dynamic programming
- greedy
- heaps and priority queues
- strings

## In one line

It is a gate, not a practice to perfect. Once you clear it, put your energy back into what actually decides what you can build.
